The Origin of Dinosaur Science

England, 1824: Reverend William Buckland & The Megalosaurus

The prehistoric reptiles known as dinosaurs arose during the Middle to Late Triassic Period of the Mesozoic Era, roughly 230 million years ago. They were members of a broader archosaurian lineage—"ruling reptiles"—which also gave rise to crocodilians and pterosaurs.

Humanity first formally studied dinosaurs during the 1820s, when naturalist William Buckland described fossilized jaws and limbs discovered in Stonesfield slate quarries in Oxfordshire, dubbing the formidable creature Megalosaurus ("great lizard").

1842: Coining "Dinosauria"

Sir Richard Owen defines an entirely new taxonomic superorder

In 1842, Sir Richard Owen, Britain's premier comparative anatomist, analyzed fossils from three distinct creatures: Megalosaurus, Iguanodon ("iguana tooth", discovered by Gideon and Mary Ann Mantell), and the armored Hylaeosaurus ("woodland lizard").

Owen realized that unlike modern lizards whose legs splay laterally, these prehistoric giants bore columnar legs positioned directly beneath their torso, like modern mammals and birds. They possessed fused sacral vertebrae in their pelvic girdles and massive skeletal architecture.

Owen synthesized the ancient Greek terms deinos ("terrible, wondrous, awe-inspiring") and sauros ("lizard or reptile") to create Dinosauria.

Pangaean Supercontinent

When dinosaurs emerged, Earth's continents formed the singular supercontinent Pangaea surrounded by Panthalassa. The tectonic splitting during the Jurassic created diverse oceanic barriers, driving extraordinary evolutionary endemism.

The Chicxulub Impactor

Sixty-six million years ago, a 10-kilometer asteroid struck the Yucatán Peninsula (Chicxulub crater), unleashing megatsunamis, global wildfires, and an impact winter that terminated non-avian dinosaurs along with 75% of Earth's species.

Dinosaurs Among Us Today

Phylogenetic connection to modern Aves

Dinosauria never truly vanished. Rigorous cladistic analysis, feather preservation in Liaoning shales, and wishbone (furcula) homologies demonstrate that all modern birds are living, feathered theropod dinosaurs.

From peregrine falcons to hummingbirds, over 10,000 avian dinosaur species flourish today—surpassing the species diversity of modern mammals.
